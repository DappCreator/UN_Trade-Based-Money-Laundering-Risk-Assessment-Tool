# Annex III: Quality of the Trade-Based Money-Laundering Control Landscape

## Purpose

Annex III provides the variable reference for assessing the quality and effectiveness of a jurisdiction's trade-based money-laundering (TBML) control landscape. It covers national strategy, domestic and international cooperation, corruption controls, trade facilitators, reporting intermediaries, customs, financial intelligence units (FIUs), investigators, prosecutors, the judiciary and free-trade-zone (FTZ)-specific controls.

The assessment concerns the quality, implementation, reach and outcomes of controls. A control should not receive a high rating merely because a law, policy or system exists; assessors should consider whether it functions in practice and reduces TBML exposure.

## Rating scale

Control variables use a 0.0–1.0 effectiveness scale:

| Score | Interpretation |
|---:|---|
| 1.0 | Excellent |
| 0.9 | Close to excellent |
| 0.8 | Very high |
| 0.7 | High |
| 0.6 | Medium-high |
| 0.5 | Medium |
| 0.4 | Medium-low |
| 0.3 | Low |
| 0.2 | Very low |
| 0.1 | Minimal |
| 0.0 | Does not exist |

For variables applicable to both mainland and FTZs, separate ratings should be assigned where the tool requires differentiated analysis.

## Strategic, cooperation and integrity controls

### 1. National commitment, policy and coordination

Assess whether the jurisdiction has a formal TBML risk assessment, national priorities, a documented strategy and action plan, high-level government endorsement, sufficient resources, tailored guidance and a functioning inter-agency task force. Effective arrangements include private-sector consultation, public communication of relevant findings and mechanisms for periodically evaluating and improving coordination.

**Evidence may include:** national AML/CFT strategies and TBML risk assessments, laws defining agency roles, task-force minutes, resource-allocation records, coordination evaluations, mutual-evaluation findings and private-sector feedback.

### 2. Domestic cooperation and information-sharing

Assess operational cooperation among FIUs, customs, tax, port, regulatory, supervisory, investigative and prosecutorial authorities. Effective systems enable secure and timely information exchange, joint investigations, interoperable or automated data systems, private-sector participation with appropriate safeguards, and practical access by FIUs and law enforcement to customs data.

For FTZs, assess reporting obligations for zone management and operators, channels for reporting suspicious activity, and cooperation with industry bodies to disengage from non-compliant zones.

**Evidence may include:** legislation, memorandums of understanding, task-force outcomes, joint investigations, referral statistics, IT architecture, audits, public-private forums, awareness materials and FTZ reporting procedures.

### 3. International cooperation and information-sharing

Assess whether the jurisdiction has effective legal gateways, competent authorities and points of contact for bilateral and multilateral cooperation. Consider participation in the Egmont Group, FATF or FATF-style bodies, WCO, WTO and OECD; spontaneous and requested information exchanges; secure communication channels; joint investigations; typology work; and monitoring of response quality and timeliness.

**Evidence may include:** treaties, customs mutual-assistance agreements, MOUs, exchange statistics, cooperation-meeting records, joint-operation results, typology exercises and lessons-learned reviews.

### 4. Control of corruption

Assess anti-corruption laws, enforcement and integrity systems affecting customs, tax, ports, shipping registries, FTZ authorities, licensing bodies, law enforcement, prosecutors and courts. Strong controls include integrity-risk assessments, asset declarations, internal investigations, whistle-blower protection, transparent licensing and procurement, effective sanctions and evidence of addressing corruption linked to trade manipulation or TBML.

**Evidence may include:** governance indicators, corruption indices, integrity strategies, prosecutions and disciplinary records, audits, procurement data, whistle-blower statistics, media investigations and case examples.

## Trade-facilitator controls

### 5. Knowledge of trade facilitators on TBML

Assess whether freight forwarders, shipping agents, customs brokers, bonded-warehouse operators and similar actors understand TBML typologies, red flags, FTZ risks and appropriate responses. Consider regular targeted training, practical guidance, professional certification, sector outreach, voluntary learning and cooperation with competent authorities.

### 6. Integrity of trade facilitators

Assess ethical conduct, internal anti-fraud controls, staff vetting, segregation of duties, whistle-blowing, audits, resistance to client pressure, professional standards and disciplinary consequences. Review evidence of collusion, false documentation, ignored red flags or other facilitator involvement in TBML and related crime.

### 7. Controls and oversight over trade facilitators

Assess registration, licensing and entry checks; identification of directors, shareholders and beneficial owners; risk-based supervision; inspections and audits; and proportionate, effective and dissuasive sanctions for misconduct, document forgery and TBML-relevant violations.

### 8. Record-keeping, monitoring and cooperation of trade facilitators

Assess whether facilitators retain complete and tamper-resistant trade records, verify documents and counterparties, monitor customers and transactions for red flags, escalate alerts and cooperate promptly with FIUs, customs, tax and law enforcement. Controls should be proportionate to the facilitator's size, role and risk exposure.

## Reporting-intermediary controls

### 9. Integrity of management and staff

Assess ethical culture, leadership, codes of conduct, vetting, staff rotation, anti-corruption controls, whistle-blowing, internal audits, accountability and prompt disciplinary action. Consider whether staff in trade finance and related functions resist collusion, undue influence and wilful blindness.

### 10. Human resources for TBML controls

Assess whether staffing, qualifications, role definitions, escalation structures, budgets and external expertise are proportionate to the intermediary's trade-related volume, complexity and risk profile. Consider recruitment, retention and periodic capacity reviews.

### 11. Supervision of reporting intermediaries

Assess the legal mandate, risk-based approach, frequency, intensity and expertise of AML/CFT supervision for trade-exposed intermediaries. Effective supervision includes TBML guidance, thematic inspections, follow-up, remediation, sanctions and sharing of relevant findings with competent authorities.

### 12. Sanctions against reporting intermediaries

Assess whether administrative and criminal sanctions for TBML-related failures are clear, proportionate, effective and dissuasive, and whether they are imposed in practice for failures involving customer due diligence, monitoring, record-keeping and STR/SAR reporting. Review enforcement outcomes and whether lessons are communicated to the sector.

### 13. Knowledge of staff of reporting intermediaries on TBML

Assess role-specific knowledge among front-line, operations, trade-finance and compliance staff. Training should cover domestic risks, typologies, red flags, trade-document verification, shipping anomalies, dual-use goods, FTZ exposure, case studies and practical STR/SAR application. Training should be tracked, evaluated and updated.

### 14. Customer due diligence, monitoring and record-keeping

Assess risk-based CDD and enhanced due diligence, transaction monitoring, sanctions and vessel or container screening, proportionate IT systems, ongoing customer review, accessible records, timely and high-quality STR/SAR reporting, data security and cooperation with FIUs and supervisors.

## Customs controls

### 15. Information-technology-based monitoring at customs

Assess integrated customs systems, complete declaration data, third-party and foreign data cross-checks, historical trade analysis, automated risk engines, TBML indicators, anomaly detection, calibrated targeting, post-clearance analytics and interoperability with tax authorities, FIUs and other agencies.

For FTZs, consider customs access to operator records, monitoring of goods, people, entities and vehicles, non-compliance histories, enhanced scrutiny of high-risk zones and publication of useful annual FTZ activity indicators.

### 16. Physical detection tools at customs

Assess the availability, coverage, maintenance and risk-based use of scanners, radiation detectors, chemical analysers, sniffer dogs and other inspection equipment. Detection results should feed electronic risk systems and be supported by documented procedures and audit trails.

For FTZs, assess continuous customs supervision of perimeters and entry or exit points, unimpeded physical access, inspection powers and enforcement of restrictions on goods, operators and persons.

### 17. Knowledge and skills of customs staff on TBML

Assess regular and risk-based training in TBML typologies, indicators, trade documentation, smuggling methods, FTZ operations, analytical and physical tools, domestic cases and practical simulations. Consider inter-agency training, expert instruction, post-training evaluation and professional certification.

### 18. Human resources at customs

Assess whether staffing and deployment reflect trade volumes, high-risk goods, corridors and FTZ exposure. Consider specialized TBML units, mandates, trained personnel, performance measures, periodic workforce reviews and retention strategies for high-risk enforcement roles.

### 19. Fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls at customs

Assess recruitment and periodic vetting, ethics training, whistle-blower channels, internal investigations, asset and interest declarations, segregation of duties, data protection, rotation of sensitive staff, disciplinary reporting, external oversight and enforcement against corrupt officials.

## FIU controls

### 20. Fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls for FIU staff

Assess documented recruitment and promotion vetting, background and financial checks, ethics obligations, independent internal oversight, sanctions, whistle-blower protection, integrity-risk assessments, staff rotation and external accountability. These safeguards should protect sensitive intelligence and analytical independence.

### 21. Knowledge and skills of FIU staff on TBML

Assess expertise in trade operations, trade finance, customs valuation, shipping records, supply chains, tax and logistics; use of trade-data tools; TBML-specific procedures; prioritization of STRs/SARs; typology development; and access to external specialists when necessary. Training should be continuous and institutionalized.

### 22. Resources and tools of the FIU

Assess dedicated TBML functions, access to customs, tax, shipping, trade, company and financial data, advanced analytics, secure case management, predictable funding, budget autonomy and staff retention. Resources should match the jurisdiction's TBML risk and workload.

## Investigation controls

### 23. Fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls for investigators

Assess periodic integrity vetting, ethics rules, internal oversight, independence from political or commercial influence, rotation, case reviews, multiple-level approvals, secure information handling and effective disciplinary action for corruption or interference in TBML cases.

### 24. Knowledge and skills of investigators on TBML

Assess specialized training, continuous development, trade and trade-finance expertise, customs valuation, logistics, taxation, use of analytical tools, access to subject-matter experts, case-learning systems and TBML-specific investigative procedures.

### 25. Resources for investigators

Assess the number and skills of investigators, compensation and retention, secure databases, trade and financial records, analytics, operational funding, forensic and expert support, cross-border logistics and planning based on workload, complexity and risk.

## Prosecution controls

### 26. Fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls for prosecutors

Assess ongoing vetting, financial and conflict-of-interest disclosures, ethics controls, internal audits, whistle-blower channels, training, accountability, external oversight and consistent enforcement across national and subnational prosecution services.

### 27. Knowledge and skills of prosecutors on TBML

Assess knowledge of international trade, customs, trade finance, supply chains, invoice fraud, transfer pricing, evidentiary requirements, asset tracing and international cooperation. Consider specialized training, institutional guidance, case management, audits and outcomes.

### 28. Resources for prosecutors

Assess specialized units, staffing and caseloads, dedicated funding, paralegal and forensic support, secure digital infrastructure, evidence-sharing protocols, liaison prosecutors, mutual legal assistance capacity, compensation and retention mechanisms.

## Judiciary controls

### 29. Fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls for the judiciary

Assess transparent judicial appointments, integrity and financial vetting, codes of conduct, mandatory ethics training, periodic reviews, independent discipline, cooperation with anti-corruption bodies, publication of appropriate performance information and dissuasive sanctions.

### 30. Knowledge and skills of judges on TBML

Assess mandatory TBML and financial-crime training, trade-document and evidence analysis, specialist panels, knowledge of AML/CFT, customs, tax, trade-finance and confiscation law, access to technical experts, international cooperation and review of judgments for learning gaps.

### 31. Resources for judges

Assess court budgets, manageable workloads, secure case-management systems, evidence storage, audiovisual and interpretation capacity, legal research support, physical security, fair compensation, retention and succession planning for complex TBML adjudication.

## FTZ-specific controls based on the OECD clean-zone framework

### 32. Unimpeded access for competent authorities

Assess whether customs, police, tax, fiscal and regulatory authorities have immediate, unconditional and practical access to FTZ premises, operators, goods, inventories and documents without prior approval or obstruction. Consider contracts, physical facilities, on-site presence and operational experience.

### 33. Activity disclosure and risk-based exclusions

Assess advance notification of planned activities, goods, operators and business-model changes; complete and current FTZ registers; beneficial-ownership information; and powers to deny or revoke access for non-compliant or high-risk actors.

### 34. Digital inventory and transaction records

Assess whether FTZ operators maintain complete, standardized, time-stamped and tamper-resistant digital records of goods, services, transactions, ownership, value, routes and location, with real-time competent-authority access and centralized FTZ aggregation.

### 35. Data exchange with competent authorities

Assess timely, complete, standardized and secure transmission of FTZ records, automated interfaces, shared platforms, prompt responses to investigations and measurable data quality, timeliness and completeness.

### 36. FTZ liaison mechanism for regulatory and enforcement cooperation

Assess whether each FTZ has a qualified, authorized and accessible point of contact, documented communications, rapid responses and backup arrangements to maintain continuity during absence or turnover.

### 37. Electronic payment mechanisms within FTZs

Assess rules and infrastructure that reduce cash, encourage electronic payments, link transactions to verifiable digital records, report cash above thresholds and require operators to disclose payment mechanisms during authorization and review.

### 38. Training and peer learning

Assess institutionalized training for FTZ management and operators, participation in national, regional and international peer-learning forums, cooperation with competent authorities and regular integration of lessons into compliance and strategic-development programmes.

## Evidence and scoring practice

For every variable, the working group should record the participants, evidence reviewed, rating rationale, caveats, deficiencies and follow-up actions. Ratings should reflect actual effectiveness and outcomes, not only formal compliance. Mainland and FTZ scores should be kept distinct wherever the tool requires separate assessment.
