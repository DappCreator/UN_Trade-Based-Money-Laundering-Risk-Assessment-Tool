# Trade-Based Money-Laundering Risk Assessment Tool

## Self-Assessment Guidance Manual

**Publisher:** United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC)  
**Co-developer:** World Bank Group  
**Edition:** Advance copy  
**Year:** 2026

## Executive summary

The UNODC–World Bank Trade-Based Money-Laundering (TBML) Risk Assessment Tool is a self-assessment framework for national authorities to evaluate and mitigate money-laundering risks arising from cross-border trade in goods. It is designed for multi-agency working groups involving customs, financial intelligence units (FIUs), law enforcement, tax authorities, prosecutors, financial-sector regulators and free-trade-zone (FTZ) authorities.

The tool helps jurisdictions:

1. Understand exposure to TBML threats in the mainland and FTZs.
2. Identify structural, sectoral and institutional vulnerabilities.
3. Assess the effectiveness of existing prevention, detection, investigation, prosecution and adjudication controls.
4. Formulate prioritized policy, supervisory, operational and capacity-building responses.

The tool complements the World Bank national money-laundering and terrorist-financing risk assessment framework. It can be used in parallel with a broader national risk assessment, as a supplementary module when TBML is a priority, or independently for a focused examination of trade-related laundering risks.

## Scope and boundaries

The tool focuses on **money-laundering risks connected with international trade in goods**. It excludes:

- Trade in services and other intangible assets.
- Domestic-only trade-based laundering.
- Comparative risk assessments of particular goods or commodities.
- Terrorist financing, proliferation financing or sanctions evasion when occurring independently of money-laundering.

The tool is intentionally sector-neutral. It can be applied to different categories of merchandise without assuming that any particular product is inherently higher risk.

Trade anomalies—including misinvoicing, multiple invoicing, misdescription, misclassification and phantom shipments—are warning indicators, not proof of TBML. The same indicators can arise in customs fraud, tax evasion, sanctions evasion, proliferation financing, terrorist financing or capital flight.

## What is TBML?

The manual uses the FATF formulation: TBML is the process of disguising the proceeds of crime and moving value through trade transactions to legitimize their illicit origins.

In practical terms, TBML uses real or fabricated trade to move value rather than simply to move goods. Commercial paperwork and payment flows may appear legitimate while actors manipulate prices, quantities, descriptions, routes, counterparties or payment terms.

### Core methods

- **Over- and under-invoicing:** manipulating prices relative to arm’s-length values to transfer value across borders.
- **Over- and under-shipment:** declaring quantities, weights or volumes that do not match the goods actually moved.
- **Multiple invoicing:** reusing invoices, bills of lading or packing lists to obtain duplicate financing or payments.
- **False description or misclassification:** misstating product quality, brand, grade or Harmonized System code.
- **Phantom shipments:** creating documents and payments for goods that were never shipped.
- **Cash integration through trade:** linking criminal cash to inventory purchases, exports, deposits or settlements under trade documentation.

### Common enabling techniques

- Third-party payments and complex fund routing.
- Shell companies, opaque ownership structures, FTZs and trans-shipment hubs.
- Abuse of letters of credit, documentary collections, open-account terms and other trade-finance instruments.
- Carousel trading and round-tripping of goods, invoices or payments.

TBML should be distinguished from trade predicate offences. A predicate offence concerns illegality in the trade itself—for example, customs fraud, smuggling, tax evasion, sanctions breaches or diversion of controlled goods. TBML concerns using trade to move, disguise or integrate criminal proceeds. Both dimensions may occur in the same case and should be investigated through coordinated trade and laundering tracks.

## Methodological framework

The model contains more than 70 variables arranged in a tree-like network:

- **Input variables** are assessed and rated by the working group.
- **Intermediate variables** are calculated automatically from input variables, weights and model assumptions.

The assessment uses a standardized 0.0–1.0 scale:

- For threats and vulnerabilities, a higher score means higher risk.
- For the control landscape, a higher score means stronger mitigation.

Residual risk is derived from three components:

1. TBML threats.
2. Inherent vulnerabilities.
3. Effectiveness of the TBML control landscape.

The network aggregates results for the mainland, FTZs and the jurisdiction as a whole. The overall jurisdictional result combines mainland and FTZ results using the model's treatment of GDP contribution and a “weakest link” approach. Weights, prerequisites and other assumptions are embedded in the spreadsheet; any amendments should be justified and documented.

## Modules

### Module 1: TBML threat assessment

Measures the scale and nature of exposure to TBML activity, including:

- Detected TBML activity and its sophistication.
- Domestic and foreign sources of criminal proceeds.
- Economic and regulatory incentives that may encourage misuse of trade systems.
- Separate exposure in the mainland and FTZs.

A high threat score does not necessarily mean that domestic controls are weak; it reflects the underlying exposure to criminal incentives and laundering demand.

### Module 2: Inherent vulnerability assessment

Evaluates structural and contextual conditions that may be exploited regardless of the immediate presence of a known laundering threat. It may cover:

- High trade volumes and complex supply chains.
- Reliance on trade facilitators and paper-based processes.
- Shadow banking, informal value transfer and cash-economy conditions.
- Sectoral dependencies and high-risk industries.
- Technological, regulatory and institutional gaps.
- FTZ-specific operating conditions.

### Module 3: TBML control landscape

Assesses the quality and effectiveness of measures to prevent, detect and respond to TBML. It covers national policy and coordination, data-sharing, integrity and resourcing of competent authorities, supervision of reporting intermediaries and oversight of trade facilitators.

Relevant control areas include:

- National commitment, policy and coordination.
- Domestic and international information-sharing.
- Control of corruption.
- Trade-facilitator knowledge, integrity, records, monitoring and oversight.
- Reporting-intermediary supervision, sanctions, customer due diligence, record-keeping and resources.
- Customs IT monitoring, physical detection tools, staffing, skills and internal controls.
- FIU integrity, skills, resources, tools and analytical capacity.
- Investigative, prosecutorial and judicial independence, integrity, skills, staffing and resources.

### Optional Module 4: comparative FTZ risk analysis

The optional FTZ module supports comparative analysis of individual free trade zones. It considers:

- Inherent risk factors.
- Risk-mitigation factors.
- Differences in governance, transparency, operator accountability, records, access and oversight.

The FTZ control baseline reflects the OECD Council Recommendation on Countering Illicit Trade: Enhancing Transparency in Free Trade Zones and the Code of Conduct for Clean Free Trade Zones.

## Working group

A working group of approximately 10–20 members is recommended. Core participants should include:

- Customs authorities.
- FIUs.
- Law enforcement agencies.
- Banking supervisors or financial-sector regulators.
- Prosecution services.
- Tax and revenue authorities.
- FTZ authorities, where applicable.

Other members may include port agencies, trade ministries and national statistical authorities.

The group should collectively possess knowledge of international trade operations, customs procedures, AML/CFT frameworks, FATF standards, data analysis, trade finance, institutional cooperation and policy development.

### Collaboration practices

Effective assessments require:

- Adequate time and institutional support for all members.
- Inclusive and balanced dialogue.
- Structured documentation of evidence, disagreements and decisions.
- Candid identification of weaknesses and operational gaps.
- A clear rationale and evidence base for every rating.
- Protection of sensitive working information where appropriate.

The assessment is intended primarily to protect the jurisdiction's economy and population; publication is not required.

## Step-by-step assessment process

### Step 1: Data identification and collection

Collect representative, current and sufficiently granular data before scoring. Data should be validated across multiple sources where possible.

Potential sources include:

- FIU STR/SAR trends, strategic analysis and typologies.
- Customs declarations, risk-management outputs, audits, seizures and inspection records.
- Law-enforcement and prosecutorial case records.
- Tax audits and enforcement results.
- Trade statistics, mirror statistics and foreign-exchange policy documents.
- AML/CFT strategies, supervisory reports and sanctions data.
- Beneficial-ownership information and trade-finance records.
- International research and standards from FATF, IMF, OECD, UNODC, UNCTAD and the WCO.

### Step 2: Assess TBML threats

Assess detected activity, criminal proceeds, typologies, trade corridors and contextual incentives in the mainland and FTZs. Ratings should distinguish known evidence from expert judgment and document data limitations.

### Step 3: Assess inherent vulnerabilities

Examine the jurisdiction's trade ecosystem, institutional design, sectors, channels, documentation, technology, informal finance and FTZ conditions. The focus is on conditions that could be exploited, not solely on known cases.

### Step 4: Evaluate the control landscape

Assess whether controls are available, properly designed, adequately resourced, implemented and effective. Consider prevention, monitoring, detection, investigation, prosecution, adjudication, sanctions, asset recovery and cooperation.

### Step 5: Draw conclusions and develop an action plan

Use the results as a diagnostic foundation for a risk-based action plan. Actions should have clear priorities, owners, timelines, resources and measures of progress.

## Data and policy implications

Quality inputs drive quality outputs. Jurisdictions should avoid unsupported optimism, recognize uncertainty and update the assessment as trade patterns, regulations, technologies and criminal typologies change.

Recommended policy outcomes include:

- Stronger cooperation among customs, FIUs, law enforcement, prosecutors, tax authorities, regulators, FTZ administrations and private-sector stakeholders.
- Interoperable systems linking customs declarations and risk profiles with financial data and FIU outputs.
- Better capacity to analyse trade documentation, financial flows and complex ownership structures.
- Stronger investigative and prosecutorial strategies for cases involving both trade offences and laundering.
- Enhanced FTZ governance based on transparent records, real-time access for competent authorities, on-site inspection rights and accountable management.
- Targeted training, staffing, analytical tools and technical assistance.
