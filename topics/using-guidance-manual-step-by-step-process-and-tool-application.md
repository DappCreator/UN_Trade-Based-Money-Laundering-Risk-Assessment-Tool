# Using the TBML Guidance Manual: Step-by-Step Process and Tool Application

## Purpose

Chapter 5 of the *Self-Assessment Guidance Manual for Trade-Based Money-Laundering Risk Assessment Tool* is both a methodological reference and a practical implementation aid. It is designed to be used alongside the Excel-based TBML Risk Assessment Tool.

The most important work is not entering scores into the spreadsheet. It is assessing input variables objectively, analysing the reasons and evidence behind each rating, documenting decisions, and interpreting how individual factors affect residual risk through the tool's causal network.

## Five-step assessment cycle

1. **Data identification and collection**
2. **Assessment of TBML threats**
3. **Assessment of TBML inherent vulnerabilities**
4. **Evaluation of the TBML control landscape**
5. **Conclusions and action plan**

## How to use the manual with the Excel tool

### Start with the manual

Each chapter provides the methodological foundation for the corresponding sections of the Excel tool. It explains what to assess, why it matters and how to interpret results.

### Use the variable templates

Each input variable is accompanied by a standardized template containing:

- A definition and scope.
- Assessment criteria.
- Possible sources of evidence.
- A rating scale from 0.0 to 1.0.

The rating scale has different meanings by module:

- In the threat and inherent-vulnerability modules, **0.0 means does not exist** and **1.0 means most extreme**.
- In the control-landscape module, **0.0 means does not exist** and **1.0 means excellent**.

Thus, higher threat and vulnerability scores indicate greater exposure, while higher control scores indicate stronger mitigation.

Templates should be the foundation for group discussion and should be supported by data and documentary evidence wherever possible.

### Populate the Excel tool

After the working group agrees on a consensus rating for each input variable, enter the score directly into the relevant spreadsheet module. The tool automatically calculates weighted outputs, aggregates scores and generates a visual risk landscape.

### Interpret the results

The tool produces an impact map that helps identify risk areas, control gaps and priorities for mitigation. Results should be interpreted through the causal chains connecting input variables to intermediate variables and residual risk, rather than treated as standalone scores.

## Tool-folder templates and module relationships

The tool folder contains six templates. Modules 1, 2, 3.a and 3.b are the core assessment modules. Their outputs feed into the **Master Template for Overall TBML Risk**.

- **Module 1:** TBML threats.
- **Module 2:** Inherent TBML vulnerabilities.
- **Module 3.a:** TBML control landscape for the mainland.
- **Module 3.b:** TBML control landscape for FTZs.
- **Master Template:** Overall TBML risk.
- **Module 4:** Optional comparative risk analysis of multiple FTZs.

Jurisdictions without FTZs may complete only the mainland sections and transfer those results to the master template. Module 4 is optional and independent from the master template: where used, its findings may support Module 3.b and relevant parts of Modules 1 and 2, but Module 4 does not directly feed the master template.

## Documentation and transparency

Every assessment decision should record:

- Who participated in the discussion.
- The data and documents reviewed.
- Key arguments, assumptions and caveats.
- Deficiencies, weaknesses and gaps.
- The final rating and its justification.

The blank working-paper template in Annex V can be used for each assessed variable. A transparent record preserves institutional memory, supports quality assurance and makes future reassessments more consistent.

## Anticipated questions and recommended responses

### What if data are unavailable?

Use expert judgment based on available documentation, stakeholder interviews or proxy indicators. Clearly document the rationale, limitations and uncertainty. Significant data gaps should become follow-up actions in the action plan.

### What if stakeholders disagree?

Use structured discussion and evidence review to seek consensus. If consensus cannot be reached, document the different positions, their justifications, minority views and any significant dissent. If a majority rating is used, record the rationale and unresolved concerns in the assessment log or annex.

### Must all variables be assessed in one session?

No. The assessment can be conducted over multiple sessions. Maintain continuity by recording decisions, evidence and justifications after each session.

### How should the assessment be validated?

Consider peer review, external consultation, validation workshops and comparison with national or sectoral risk assessments. Anonymous perception surveys of relevant agency staff and private stakeholders may provide a useful cross-check.

### How can unnecessary detail be avoided?

Prioritize material factors. Focus on systemic weaknesses, enforcement gaps and high-risk sectors rather than seeking perfect data for every variable.

## Step 1: Data identification and collection

Step 1 establishes the empirical foundation for the entire assessment. Data should be representative, disaggregated where possible, current, legally shareable and validated across multiple sources.

### Data-collection recommendations

- Use multisource triangulation, combining quantitative and qualitative sources such as STR statistics and typology reports.
- Aim for a three- to five-year time series where feasible.
- Ground the analysis in local predicate crimes, trade structures and institutional realities.
- Address confidentiality and access constraints that affect inter-agency sharing.

### Map trade flows, sectors and FTZs

Collect import and export volumes by sector, origin, destination and transport mode. Use customs, trade-ministry and port-authority data to identify trade hubs, chokepoints and high-volume corridors. Map FTZs, their regulatory frameworks and whether they fall within the national AML/CFT regime. Include private-sector information and audit findings on mispricing or high-risk product categories.

### Gather case and reporting data

Request detailed FIU STR/SAR statistics on trade-related filings, including incoming and outgoing reports. Gather TBML case numbers, values, methods and sectors; law-enforcement and prosecution data; asset-recovery and conviction outcomes; mutual legal assistance requests; and spontaneous disclosures from foreign counterparts. Record whether detection was proactive, such as risk profiling, or reactive, such as a foreign alert.

### Compile typologies and implementation arrangements

Collect domestic strategic and operational reports from FIUs, customs and law enforcement, and supplement them with FATF, Egmont Group and Asia/Pacific Group on Money Laundering material. Include sector-specific red flags.

Implementation should include a shared data-collection template, assigned agency leads for variables and modules, technical meetings for data-gap validation, secure legally compliant data-sharing platforms, and cooperation with academia where useful.

## Step 2: Assessment of TBML threats

Step 2 evaluates the extent to which illicit actors exploit international trade to move, obscure and integrate criminal proceeds, and the conditions that make the jurisdiction attractive for laundering schemes.

The working group should consider:

- Whether domestic criminals use trade to move funds abroad.
- Whether foreign criminal organizations use the jurisdiction as a conduit or destination.
- The scale and sophistication of detected TBML activity.
- Domestic and external sources of criminal proceeds.
- Economic and regulatory conditions that create laundering incentives.
- The distinction between outward TBML, where domestic proceeds move abroad, and inward TBML, where external proceeds enter through trade.

Threat ratings should reflect both indicators and detection capacity. A high number of detected cases can mean high exposure, strong enforcement, or both. Interpret case data alongside the control-landscape assessment.

### Threat variables

#### Mainland TBML threat

- Investigated TBML cases in the mainland.
- Volume of proceeds from organized or serious crime in the mainland.
- Volume of criminal proceeds in major trade counterparts.
- Contextual pressures such as exchange controls, economic instability and high taxation.
- Weak beneficial-ownership and transparency requirements.
- Porous borders and cross-border barter trade.
- Government subsidies for certain goods.
- Tax incentives.
- Aggressive trade-liberalization policies.

#### FTZ-related TBML threat

- Investigated TBML cases in FTZs.
- Volume of criminal proceeds in major FTZ trade counterparts.
- Weak beneficial-ownership and transparency requirements in FTZs.
- Tax incentives in FTZs.
- Aggressive trade-liberalization policies in FTZs.

“Significant” should be interpreted in national context, using benchmarks such as trade-to-GDP ratios, customs-declaration volumes, the share of TBML cases among money-laundering cases, TBML case values relative to imports and exports, and the share of trade-linked STRs/SARs.

## Step 3: Assessment of inherent vulnerabilities

Step 3 identifies structural weaknesses and characteristics of the trade, financial, institutional and regulatory environment that could be exploited if threats exist. It assesses exposure to misuse, not actual misuse.

### Mainland vulnerability variables

- Shadow banking or informal credit facilities.
- Informal value-transfer systems.
- Heavy reliance on cash transactions.
- High trade volume.
- Complex or opaque supply chains.
- Dependence on trade facilitators.
- Dependence on corridors prone to smuggling.
- Reliance on paper-based documentation and procedures.
- Dependence on industries recognized internationally as vulnerable to TBML, such as gold, textiles or used cars.
- Use of vessels with no or illegal registration.
- Extent of virtual-asset-based trade settlements.

Shadow banking or informal credit facilities concern unregulated credit provision; informal value-transfer systems concern payment and settlement mechanisms. They are related but distinct variables.

### FTZ vulnerability variables

- Economic and trade significance of FTZs.
- Nature of permitted activities, including repackaging, processing and trans-shipment.
- Complex or opaque supply chains.

The analysis should account for each jurisdiction's trade environment, economic characteristics and institutional maturity. Where data are limited, expert judgment may be used if documented and triangulated with interviews and external reports.

## Step 4: Evaluation of the control landscape

Step 4 evaluates whether controls are designed, implemented, resourced and effective in preventing, detecting and responding to TBML. Modules 3.a and 3.b assess the mainland and FTZs separately.

The controls are organized into three domains:

1. **Strategic and institutional controls** — national policies, coordination, resource planning and anti-corruption frameworks.
2. **Sectoral and operational controls** — trade facilitators, reporting intermediaries, customs, FIUs, investigators, prosecutors and the judiciary.
3. **Enabling environment and oversight** — supervision, sanctions, training, integrity protocols and risk-appropriate detection technology.

The control framework contains 31 variables. Most apply to both mainland and FTZ settings, while selected variables are separately scored for FTZs. FTZ-specific controls are based on the OECD Code of Conduct for Clean Free Trade Zones.

### National and institutional control variables

1. National commitment, policy and coordination.
2. Domestic cooperation and information-sharing.
3. International cooperation and information-sharing.
4. Control of corruption.
5. Knowledge of trade facilitators on TBML.
6. Integrity of trade facilitators.
7. Controls and oversight over trade facilitators.
8. Record-keeping, monitoring and cooperation.
9. Integrity of reporting-intermediary management and staff.
10. Human resources for TBML controls at reporting intermediaries.
11. Supervision of reporting intermediaries.
12. Sanctions against reporting intermediaries.
13. Knowledge of reporting-intermediary staff on TBML.
14. Customer due diligence, monitoring and record-keeping.
15. IT-based monitoring at customs.
16. Physical detection tools at customs.
17. Knowledge and skills of customs staff on TBML.
18. Human resources at customs.
19. Customs fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls.
20. FIU fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls.
21. Knowledge and skills of FIU staff on TBML.
22. FIU resources and tools.
23. Investigator fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls.
24. Investigator knowledge and skills on TBML.
25. Investigator resources.
26. Prosecutor fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls.
27. Prosecutor knowledge and skills on TBML.
28. Prosecutor resources.
29. Judicial fit-and-proper checks and internal corruption controls.
30. Judge knowledge and skills on TBML cases.
31. Judicial resources.

### FTZ-specific controls

- Unimpeded access for competent authorities.
- Activity disclosure and risk-based exclusion of high-risk operators.
- Digital inventory and transaction records.
- Data exchange with competent authorities.
- FTZ liaison mechanisms for regulatory and enforcement cooperation.
- Electronic payment mechanisms within FTZs.
- Training and peer learning.

Control effectiveness must be judged by practical implementation and outcomes, not merely by whether a policy or procedure exists.

## Step 5: Conclusions and action plan

After scores are entered, the tool calculates aggregated results using its internal logic model and generates an impact map. Red areas indicate higher potential impact and generally higher priority for action; green areas indicate relatively lower impact but should continue to be monitored.

The results support:

- Strategic prioritization of reforms and investment.
- Technical-assistance and policy planning.
- Communication with non-technical stakeholders.
- Development of a concise public or sanitized report and, where necessary, a confidential extended report.

The action plan should convert findings into concrete, measurable and coordinated action. Possible actions include:

- Legislative and regulatory reforms.
- Reallocation or augmentation of frontline and analytical resources.
- Stronger domestic and international information-sharing platforms.
- Operational measures to prevent, detect, investigate and prosecute TBML.
- Specialized training and awareness for high-risk sectors and actors.
- Public-private task forces, joint typology research, red-flag guidance and tailored reporting protocols.

Each action should identify a responsible institution, priority, timeline, resources, dependencies and an indicator of progress.

## Communication and dissemination

Public and private stakeholders should share a common understanding of the findings. Jurisdictions should plan how to disseminate results through sanitized reports, practical briefings and workshops tailored to FIUs, customs, law enforcement, tax authorities, supervisors, trade ministries, trade facilitators, financial institutions and other relevant stakeholders.
